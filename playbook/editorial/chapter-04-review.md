# Editorial Review — Chapter 4: Impersonation Latency

*Review pass following the first complete draft.*

## Overall assessment

The chapter discharges Part I's two outstanding debts: it delivers the
measurement methodology Chapter 1 promised ("what is unmeasured cannot be
managed"), and it resolves the tension Chapter 3's review flagged by
explicitly splitting the *measured metric* (disclosure latency) from the
*named problem* (the full three-question window). That shorthand — stated
once and reused deliberately — is the chapter's most important editorial
decision, and every later chapter that cites pilot data must respect it.
"What the Metric Honestly Cannot See" is the credibility keystone of the
whole book: it concedes the covert-agent blind spot in plain terms before
any critic can raise it, and converts the concession into the framework's
actual strategy (make disclosure the cheap default).

## Critique

1. **The two-calls opening is efficient but thinner than Chapters 1–3's
   scenes.** It is a constructed contrast rather than a lived incident. This
   is defensible — the chapter is the metrical hinge of the book and earns
   its abstraction — but it breaks the streak of scenario-first openings
   drawn from operational texture. Accepted as-is; do not let Chapters 5+
   cite it as license to open with diagrams.
2. **"The vendor who asks to be measured" is a projection, not an
   observation.** No such vendor event exists in the project materials. The
   draft frames it in the present-tense hypothetical register of the other
   examples, which blurs that line. Revision required at the consistency
   pass: mark it explicitly as an anticipated dynamic ("expect the first
   vendor who...") or move it to Chapter 18, where adoption dynamics are
   the subject. **This is the only integrity-level item in Part I.**
3. **The bimodal-distribution example similarly describes a pattern pilots
   *should look for*, not one any pilot has published.** The draft's
   Figure 4-3 caption carries the disclaimer; the body prose should carry a
   trace of it too ("a payer running the measurement may find...").
4. **Turns-versus-seconds is asserted with confidence ("turns matter more").**
   Grounded in the pilot kit's own choice of stability unit, so acceptable,
   but the argument (a turn is where data moves) deserves its one sentence
   of support — present. No change.

## Weak arguments identified

- **"Zero-latency behavior is a design choice available at no technical
  cost"** — mostly true (a disclosure sentence is configuration), but
  vendors will argue completion-rate costs: disclosed calls may get hung up
  on by the receiving side. That objection is Chapter 1's perverse incentive
  in mirror image, and the framework's real answer is that receiving-side
  policy must stop punishing disclosure (Chapter 10). Add one
  forward-pointing clause at revision.
- **The claim that a shared metric changes regulator conversations** is
  plausible but ahead of evidence — no regulator has consumed this metric.
  The sentence is framed as capability ("can be shown"), not event. Keep it
  that way.

## Transitions

- **In:** picks up "three questions on a clock" exactly as Chapter 3's
  closer promised. Verified.
- **Out:** hands Part II a clean brief — Chapter 5 must define the framework
  *and its negative space*. The Part I → Part II seam is the book's biggest
  structural joint; Chapter 5's opening should acknowledge the reader now
  carries four chapters of problem and is owed the solution's shape quickly.

## Diagram recommendations

- **Figure 4-4 (the metric card)** is the chapter's deliverable and should
  be designed for extraction — single page, self-contained, includes the
  honest-boundary statement so it cannot be photocopied into dishonesty.
- **Figure 4-3's** censored-tail treatment (never-disclosed drawn outside
  the axis) is unusual and correct; brief the illustrator on why, or it
  will be "fixed" into a final bin.
- **Figure 4-1/1-3 rhyme** re-confirmed as a paired commission.

## Verdict

Sound, with one required revision (example #2's hypothetical framing). Part
I is now complete as a unit: problem lived (1), problem explained (2),
problem structured (3), problem measured (4). Ready for the Part II pivot.
