# Editorial Review — Chapter 2: The Rise of AI Voice Agents

*Review pass following the first complete draft.*

---

## Overall assessment

The chapter executes the transition the Chapter 1 review called for: it opens
on the same call from the opposite end of the line, and the practice manager
is drawn with enough specificity (two vacancies, a growing backlog, a
rational yes) to carry the chapter's central reframe — legitimate actors,
missing infrastructure, coordination failure. That reframe is the chapter's
load-bearing argument and its best contribution: it converts "adoption ahead
of governance" from a lament into a mechanism with four identifiable parts,
each of which points at a property the framework must have. The strongest
new material is "Governance instruments watch other doors," which explains
the gap without accusing anyone of negligence, and the bot-to-bot material,
which is the book's first use of the trace library as evidence.

## Critique

1. **The chapter is analysis-heavy and scene-light after the opening.** The
   practice manager appears once and never returns. Chapter 1 kept returning
   to its opening call; this chapter's opening figure could return at least
   once — e.g., in "The delegated adopter" example, which is structurally her
   story one step further removed. Queued for revision: one returning beat,
   not a running character.

2. **Four-part structure repetition.** Both "Why Healthcare Administrative
   Calling Came First" and "Why Governance Lost the Race" are four-item
   analytical lists, and the stack section is a three-item list. The chapter
   risks reading as nested enumeration. The prose disguises it reasonably
   well (no numbered lists, varied paragraph openings), but the next
   full-manuscript pass should consider converting one section to continuous
   argument.

3. **The economics claims are directional, not quantified — keep them that
   way.** "Costs approximately nothing," "the throughput comparison is not
   close," "less than one of the salaries" are qualitative claims a reader
   will accept from reasoning alone. The draft contains no market-size
   figures, adoption percentages, or vendor pricing, and must not acquire
   them in revision unless a citable source enters the repository. This is
   the same credibility discipline flagged in the Chapter 1 review, and this
   chapter is where the temptation is strongest.

4. **Platform naming required care and got it — verify it survives
   revision.** Naming VAPI, Twilio, Vonage, Retell, and Amazon Connect is
   grounded (the repository ships adapters for exactly these) and the draft
   twice disclaims endorsement/indictment. Any future edit that attaches
   behavioral claims to a named platform ("X's agents don't disclose") must
   be rejected — no such evidence exists in the materials, and one such
   sentence would expose the book legally and reputationally.

## Weak arguments identified (and how the draft handles them)

- **"Nobody's rational decision included the externality" is an economic
  frame asserted, not derived.** A skeptic could argue payers *do* feel the
  cost and could contract it away. The draft's implicit answer — the cost
  lands on the receiving side, across an organizational boundary, with no
  contractual privity between the payer and the vendor who deployed the
  agent — is present but distributed across two sections. Acceptable;
  Chapter 16 (Risk Management) is the place to make the incidence argument
  rigorous, and a note has been left in the outline to do so.
- **The claim that governance instruments miss this channel is broad.** The
  draft names the specific instruments (security review triggers, procurement
  thresholds, HIPAA's scope, consumer robocall law) and states what each
  actually watches, which keeps it inspectable rather than sweeping. It
  stops short of legal analysis — correct for an operational playbook — but
  Chapter 19 (Standards Alignment) must not contradict the characterization
  when it maps the regulatory landscape in detail.
- **"Expect automated call volume to grow past historical baselines" is a
  prediction.** It is reasoned (removal of the human rate limit) rather than
  sourced, and it is framed as capacity-planning prudence, not forecast. The
  hedge is adequate. Do not sharpen it into a projection.

## Suggested improvements for the next revision pass

- Bring the practice manager back once, in "The delegated adopter," as noted
  above.
- "The channel's greatest strength, universal reachability, is precisely what
  made it governable by nothing" — strong sentence, but "governable by
  nothing" is doing unusual grammatical work. Candidate: "ungoverned by
  anything."
- The Executive Summary runs three paragraphs and slightly outgrows its role;
  the third paragraph (the "central claim") could compress into the second.
- Consider whether "The Stack That Made It Easy" belongs before "Why
  Healthcare Administrative Calling Came First" — technology then terrain,
  rather than terrain then technology. Current order was chosen to keep the
  chapter anchored in operations rather than technology; revisit only if
  early readers stumble.

## Transitions

- **In:** the opening explicitly re-frames Chapter 1's call from the other
  end — the exact hinge the Chapter 1 review requested. Verified.
- **Within:** the hinge from adoption mechanics to governance analysis ("It
  is tempting to treat 'adoption ahead of governance' as a lament...") works.
  The move into "Real-World Examples" is abrupt but conventional for the
  chapter template.
- **Out:** the closer sets up Chapter 3 by converting "who is calling?" into
  three questions (human? representing whom? authorized?). Chapter 3 must
  open by honoring that decomposition — it is now a promise made twice (end
  of chapter and concepts table).

## Verdict

Sound draft, safe to build on. The reframe (coordination failure, not
intrusion) is now established as the book's ideological spine — Chapters 10
(enforcement) and 16 (risk) must stay consistent with it, since both will be
tempted toward adversarial language. No structural rewrite required.
